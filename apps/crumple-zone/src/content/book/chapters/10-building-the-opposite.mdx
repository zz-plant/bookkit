## The Positive Project

How a system is built decides who absorbs its failures. The current design of work and life assumes that harm is inevitable. This is a lie. It is a design choice. We can build the opposite.

We stop routing shocks into people. We route them into buffers, slack, rollback, and coverage. The goal is to build infrastructure where these elements exist so that human bodies do not become the cheapest shock absorbers.

## Structural Mercy

The death of slack is the death of mercy. We must reinstate structural mercy. This means designing for failure. It means assuming that things will break.

Cooling capacity is underbuilt while throughput targets stay unchanged. When the system runs at one hundred percent, people become the **crumple zone**. They break before the system does. The organization preserves its own output by routing spikes into waiting, paperwork, and emotional regulation demands on individuals.

Build systems that fail safely. Build systems that absorb the shock. Do not build systems that require human resilience to function.

## The Refusability Gap

Refusal is a structural design requirement. It is not optional. When we remove the ability to say no, we introduce coercion. We must close the refusability gap.

The absence of refusal points is a political choice. Coercive outcomes result from design choices. Systems that make refusal impossible are coercive by design. Designers should bear the burden of proving non-coercion.

Refusal is not only individual. It is collective. Design for refusal. Make it easy to opt out. Make it easy to pause. Make it easy to say no without penalty. If you cannot say no, you are not free. You are a component.

Identify a system you participate in involuntarily. Where do you lack the ability to say no? What would a refusable version look like, one where participation is genuinely optional?

## Maintenance as First Class

Maintenance is invisible labor until failure. The gap between needs and resources is the deficit. This gap accumulates silently until catastrophic failure. Those who do maintenance labor are rendered invisible.

We must make maintenance visible. We must fund upkeep. We must value maintenance over production. Do not push the cost of upkeep onto the user. Do not treat ease as cheating.

Ask who does maintenance you benefit from but do not see. What in your life is being maintained that you do not notice?

## Surveillance and Universality

Universal design reduces the need for surveillance. When systems must serve everyone, the incentive to surveil and sort disappears. Means-testing requires surveillance infrastructure. It requires you to prove you deserve the baseline.

Universal systems are simpler, cheaper, and less coercive than targeted ones. They do not need to collect records to verify worthiness at every turn.

Where do you undergo eligibility verification? What would a universal version look like? Build for universality. Build for clarity. Build these as the foundation.

## Small Reversible Actions

You do not need to build a new world today. You need to build a small, reversible action.

1. Identify one point where lag clusters at human interfaces rather than machine constraints.
2. Ask who owns the buffer and how to roll back the change.
3. Name who absorbs the load if the current design fails.
4. Protect that buffer by demanding explicit rollback and clear ownership.
