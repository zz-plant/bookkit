## Claim

Justice is the accounting of who pays for stability. When a system cannot hold its own shock, it routes strain into people. The cost lands on those with no exit. Justice is the assignment of who absorbs the cost of system stability.

## Who absorbs volatility?

Institutions do not vanish problems. They move them. When buffers and slack are underbuilt and throughput targets stay fixed, the strain lands on people. The crumple zone is the outcome of underbuilt cooling capacity while throughput targets stay unchanged. Institutional stability is purchased by externalizing pressure onto human bodies.

Ask if the same people would still absorb the spike if staffed backup, explicit rollback, and clear ownership were in place. If the answer is no, the system is using people as infrastructure.

1. Track where delays accumulate. If lag clusters at human interfaces rather than machine constraints, the load is being routed into people.
2. Ask what happens when the people absorbing the load reach capacity. If they break before the system does, they are the crumple zone.
3. Compare resolution and withdrawal rates between people who can afford to wait and people who cannot. A gap indicates who pays for stability.

## Who gets reversibility?

Institutions can impose harmful states quickly. They cannot restore people to baseline at comparable speed. The reversibility gap is a structural asymmetry. Interim harm accumulates while correction is pending. This applies even when harm is brief, provided restoration is not immediate.

Compare time-to-harm with time-to-repair. Large ratios indicate a gap. Check whether benefits and access can be provisionally restored during review. If not, interim harm builds while correction is pending.

1. Compare time-to-harm with time-to-repair. Large ratios indicate a gap.
2. Check whether benefits and access can be provisionally restored during review.
3. Note the exclusion: a brief hold with automatic safeguards preserving baseline rights is not a gap.
4. Justice is the assignment of who absorbs the cost of stability when restoration trails imposition.

## How delay operates

Elapsed time is weaponized only when leverage is attached. Delay as force exhausts options and reduces claims. Wait times and review loops increase burden for the claimant while preserving institutional flexibility. Waiting raises the cost of refusal.

Delay acts as force only when it relies on fatigue, deadlines, or uncertainty to force consent. It is not a legitimate wait for missing information. It is not a neutral scheduling delay with no leverage attached. It is not predictable batching with published SLAs, proactive updates, and automatic interim protections. It is not necessary safety review with bounded timelines and accountable escalation.

Ask what would change if a firm deadline were set early. If power shifts, delay is the lever. Ask whether the delay has a repair clock with an owner, deadline, and remedy. If it has none and waiting creates new harm, delay is operating as force.

1. Track time-to-resolution and withdrawal rates by how long people can afford to wait. A gap between those who can wait and those who cannot indicates the mechanism.
2. Ask what would change if a firm deadline were set early. If power shifts, delay is the lever.
3. Ask whether the delay has a repair clock (owner, deadline, remedy). If it has none and waiting creates new harm, delay is operating as force.
4. Justice is the assignment of who pays the cost when delay is used as leverage.

## The cost of engineered dissipation

The system uses engineered dissipation to move shock elsewhere. The stability is real but paid for by people. Justice is the assignment of who pays the bill for that stability.
