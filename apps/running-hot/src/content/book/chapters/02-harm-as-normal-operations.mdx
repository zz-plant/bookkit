## Claim

Many harms persist not as exceptions but as routine output of process.

## "Pending" as governance

Pending is not a status; it is a mechanism of control. It suspends rights and obligations without providing recourse. When a case remains pending, the burden of proof and the cost of waiting fall entirely on the claimant. The institution retains flexibility while the individual loses time. This state is not accidental. It is a deliberate governance pattern that shifts risk from the system to the person.

## Policy as ownership solvent

Policy often serves as a solvent for ownership. When rules are vague or contradictory, no single actor can be held accountable for the outcome. Ambiguity allows the institution to deny responsibility while still enforcing the burden. The claimant must navigate opaque criteria without clear guidance. This lack of clarity is functional. It prevents the system from being forced to repair harm because no one can be identified as the cause.

## Delay as cost transfer

Delay operates as a cost transfer mechanism. Elapsed time exhausts options, reduces claims, and pushes people to withdraw or accept worse terms without an explicit denial. Wait times and review loops increase burden for the claimant while preserving institutional flexibility. Time costs are shifted onto people with urgent needs, unstable income, or fragile health. Waiting raises the cost of refusal, relying on fatigue, deadlines, or uncertainty to force consent.

This is not a legitimate wait for missing information. It is not a neutral scheduling delay with no leverage attached. It is not predictable batching with published SLAs, proactive updates, and automatic interim protections. It is not necessary safety review with bounded timelines and accountable escalation.

## Stability purchased by invisibility

Stability is purchased by invisibility. The system maintains its output by routing shocks and volatility into people. Unpaid labor, delay, and physiological stress absorb what buffers and slack should. Human bodies become the cheapest shock absorbers. This is engineered dissipation. The harm is real, but it is invisible to the metrics of operational success.
