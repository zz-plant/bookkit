title: "Delay as force"
definition: "A governance pattern where elapsed time is weaponized: delays exhaust options, reduce claims, and push people to withdraw or accept worse terms without an explicit denial."
is:
  - "Wait times and review loops that increase burden for the claimant while preserving institutional flexibility."
  - "Time costs shifted onto people with urgent needs, unstable income, or fragile health."
  - "Waiting that raises the cost of refusal, relying on fatigue, deadlines, or uncertainty to force consent."
isNot:
  - "A legitimate wait for missing information."
  - "A neutral scheduling delay with no leverage attached."
  - "Predictable batching with published SLAs, proactive updates, and automatic interim protections."
  - "Necessary safety review with bounded timelines and accountable escalation."
predictions:
  - "Decisions are deferred until the harmed party has fewer alternatives, and deadlines appear suddenly after long stalls."
  - "High-friction cases resolve through dropout more often than through merit decisions."
  - "Institutions report fewer denials while unresolved inventory and elapsed-harm costs rise."
  - "People with less buffer accept worse terms sooner, just to stop the waiting."
tests:
  - "Track time-to-resolution and withdrawal rates by how long people can afford to wait; a gap between those who can wait and those who cannot indicates the mechanism."
  - "Ask what would change if a firm deadline were set early; if power shifts, delay is the lever."
  - "Ask whether the delay has a repair clock (owner, deadline, remedy); if it has none and waiting creates new harm, delay is operating as force."
---

Delay can look procedural while functioning coercively. If time pressure changes what people can demand, time is part of the enforcement mechanism.
