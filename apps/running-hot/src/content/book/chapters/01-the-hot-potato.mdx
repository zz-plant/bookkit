## Claim

Obligation can be assigned without custody. Under stress, the person stays obligated while the lever needed to fix the failure is held elsewhere or by no one.

## Obligation without custody

Institutions can assign outcomes to people who control none of the levers that produce them. A worker is judged on timeliness while approvals sit elsewhere. A case is marked incomplete while system access is withheld.

This is a control mismatch. The person bears the weight of the result but cannot touch the mechanism that drives it. Failure is individualized even when the blocked lever is organizational.

A role that holds approval, budget, and escalation power is not obligation without custody, even when it fails.

Test for custody: Can the responsible person approve, unblock, or reroute work without special favors? Map one failed outcome to the lever that failed. Ask whether the person blamed could touch it. If the answer is no, custody is missing.

## How responsibility diffuses under stress

Under load, approvals or access move away from the person, and the obligation stays with them. A worker needs a signature to proceed, but the approver is on leave and no one else is authorized to sign.

Check who can approve this work. Check who can grant access. Check who sets the order of work. Can the person blamed reach any of them?

Custody is missing whenever the person blamed cannot touch the failed lever, whether someone else owns it or no one does. A short handoff with a clear owner and compensating controls does not count.

## Why "the system works" can mean no one owns repair

"The system works" often describes throughput, not stability. When repair is not owned, it is not performed.

Check who holds the repair lever. If the person held responsible cannot approve, unblock, or reroute the repair, custody is missing. The institution can report that it works while the repair lever is unowned and the person who cannot touch it is still blamed.
